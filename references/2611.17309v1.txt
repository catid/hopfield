                                                   Looped models have emerged as a prominent paradigm for reasoning, with suc-
                                                   cesses on simple reasoning puzzles such as Sudoku-Extreme and Maze-Unique.
                                                   However, these models can be unstable, slow to train, and use many parameters
                                                   and FLOPs to solve simple puzzles. Descent on an energy not only mitigates
                                                   these issues but also offers advantages: built-in early stopping, compute that adapts
                                                   to problem difficulty, fast training without differentiating through a solver, and
                                                   interpretable weights that learn the constraints of the puzzle. Here we demonstrate
                                                   this with a learned version of Hopfield & Tank’s energy from the 80s, which we
                                                   term CRAM, solves 99.996% of Sudoku-Extreme and 100% of Maze-Unique test
                                                   puzzles after 42 ms and 132 ms of training on one GPU, at least 1,000× faster than
                                                   looped models on these tasks. CRAM also matches baseline looped models at their
                                                   highest accuracy on Sudoku-Extreme using at least 282× fewer inference FLOPs.
                                                   Our work suggests two things. First, that energies are an extremely promising
                                                   direction for training reasoning from data. Second, a caution against using triv-
                                                   ially solvable benchmarks like Sudoku and Maze to test the scalability of looped
                                                   reasoning techniques.


                                         1    Introduction
                                         An emerging new paradigm for reasoning is looping, in which a layer, or block of layers, is repeatedly
                                         applied to produce an answer. Small looped models [62, 38] (millions of parameters) have had
                                         successes on simple benchmarks such as Sudoku and mazes. However, they are slow to train in part
                                         due to their use of truncated backprop-through-time (BPTT), and the reasons behind their successes
                                         remain unclear [2, 51].
                                         Energy minimization is a related framework that offers a principled approach to looping. Here,
                                         each loop step corresponds to a gradient descent step on an energy function. Minimizing energy
                                         offers many potential benefits, such as guaranteed stability, early stopping, and efficient training
                                         methods that bypass the need for BPTT. Indeed, looped reasoners have begun adopting the ideas of
                                         energy-based networks without adopting an explicit energy. Some train their updates to converge to
                                         attractors or fixed points, a guarantee intrinsic to energy network dynamics [36, 44]. Others learn
                                         when to stop with a separate halting network [28, 5, 62, 38]. Each of these desirable properties is
                                         generally added on top of the looped network through an extra head, loss, or module, and the network
                                         is trained by backpropagating through its repeated applications. With looped models rebuilding the
                                         desirable properties of an energy one at a time, we ask: why not learn the energy directly?
                                         It’s already known that constraint puzzles like Sudoku have a corresponding energy that, when
                                         minimized, solves the puzzle [34]. In the 1980’s, Hopfield and Tank showed how to hand-design an
                                         energy function that solves certain classes of logic problems [35]. It is, however, less well understood
                                         if an explicit energy can be learned from data to solve a wide array of reasoning problems.

Here, we develop a trainable version of the Hopfield-Tank energy and then learn the weights directly
from puzzles. We call this tiny trainable energy, together with the recurrent dynamics that descend it
at inference time, a Compact Reasoning Associative Memory (CRAM).We call it this because, as in
a Hopfield network [32], its weights memorize associations between pairs of valid cells, and descent
on the energy completes a corrupted pattern (i.e., a puzzle provided with only its clues). CRAM’s
energy is a special case of the energies of modern Hopfield networks [40, 41].
In this work we: 1) show that CRAM trains at least 1,000× faster than modern looped models and
reasons with at least 282× fewer inference FLOPs on a variety of different puzzles (Sudoku-Extreme
[62], Maze-Unique [36], and the N-Queens and graph-coloring sets of Baek et al. [3]); 2) show that
CRAM provides a stopping test that allows compute to scale with the difficulty of a puzzle; 3) show
that CRAM is data-efficient, learning interpretable weights from Sudoku-Extreme using an order of
magnitude fewer training puzzles than looped models; 4) show that local training objectives train
fastest compared to other methods of training the same energy — significantly faster than BPTT; and
5) show that a hand-set CRAM energy with few unique weights can solve every test puzzle from
every benchmark exactly.
CRAM represents the state of the art on speed and efficiency of looping to solve constraint puzzles, by
several orders of magnitude. However, since these puzzles can be solved easily with so few parameters
using CRAM, our work suggests that high accuracy on these benchmarks are not indicative of future
performance on harder tasks. Indeed, CRAM’s simple energy on its own would struggle to learn
more complex reasoning tasks like ARC-AGI [12], though we do show how a simple latent extension
of CRAM can solve Maze-Hard near perfectly in App. F. Nevertheless, the dramatic speed-up that
this energy provides on the presented tasks offers a potential path forward for data-efficient general
reasoning capabilities.


2   Background

CRAM’s energy is a form of pairwise graphical model in that it is a sum of costs on and between
cells, as in the energy of a Markov random field (MRF) [7, 61]. Learning these costs goes by several


                                                                            2
names, such as constraint programming [54] or constraint acquisition [9, 6, 15]. Recent methods
show that you can learn pairwise costs for Sudoku directly from solved puzzles, using approximate
likelihood methods like those studied in this work [10, 16, 17]. However, to infer solutions to new
puzzles, these methods pass the learned constraints to an external solver [13]. CRAM keeps this
learning problem but uses neural optimization methods to replace the external solver with recurrent
search on its own energy [35, 47].
Energy-based networks treat an energy over a large search space of possible solutions as the
objective of both training and inference. Training shapes the energy to be low at observed data;
inference or “reasoning” minimizes the energy to sample low-energy solutions (see Fig. 1) [32, 42].
Hopfield and Tank used a version of the Hopfield Network with continuous neurons to solve constraint
problems like the Traveling Salesman Problem with hand-set weights [33, 35], and later work did the
same for Sudoku [34]. CRAM writes these energies in a mean-field free energy form with learnable
weights [47]. This form approximates the free energy of a Boltzmann machine, which have similarly
been applied to logic and combinatorial problems [1, 30, 14, 59]. However, none of these energies
have been trained by observing puzzle solutions alone.
Modern looped reasoners use deep, weight-tied networks to learn puzzles directly from data. HRM
and TRM popularized this design for Sudoku, mazes and ARC, building on recurrent relational
networks and universal transformers [62, 38, 12, 46, 18]. Later works varied the architecture but kept
the same training approach: backpropagating through the reasoning trajectory of a depth-recurrent
model to refine the model’s prediction for a specified input [25, 3, 55, 24, 64, 39, 57, 52]. Some
approaches use diffusion- and flow-style training to avoid BPTT [21, 64, 39, 29, 65, 57]. Others
engineer fixed point behavior into deep models, training the update loop to converge to stable
equilibria [36, 44, 29, 65, 23]. This last direction holds promise, since Hopfield & Tank already
showed that these reasoning puzzles are naturally suited to fixed-point problems.
Energy-based looped models encourage convergence by training an energy on top of a deep network,
seeking to combine the best of looped models with advantages of Hopfield Networks and Boltzmann
Machines [20, 21, 27, 43, 49, 66, 11]. For example, concurrent work Energy-Based Reasoners (EBR)
[11] showcases several advantages of energy for looped model reasoning: it trains an energy on the
latent states of a TRM-style transformer, descends it with noisy projected gradient steps, and stops
when the gradient norm is small [11]. Though similar in spirit, these networks are much larger and
slower than CRAM, and all of them learn through BPTT.


3    Method

The reasoning tasks studied in this work can be represented via M answer variables called cells, each
with up to C candidate values. Therefore, an energy is assigned to each M × C belief array p, whose
(non-negative) entry pia gives the probability of candidate value a at cell i. Simply, pi is modeled as
a softmax distribution over possible answers for cell i.

with learnable parameters θ and inverse temperature β > 0. For each pair of cells i, j, the matrix

With η = 1, this is the one-step update v i ← − j W ij pj − B i , so each iteration is one layer of

We find that randomness during inference is important to help escape local minima and explore
distinct solutions, so for our experiments we use a form of Gibbs sampling known as focused Gibbs
search which prioritizes cell updates based on their local energy [35, 26]. The energy also provides a
natural early-stopping criterion: search can stop either when a board’s energy is at most a threshold
fitted from the training solutions, or when no single-cell change lowers the energy (App. C).

 4    Experiments
In our experiments, a learned energy loses no accuracy against looped models, trains at least 1,000×
faster, and uses less inference compute (Sec. 4.1). Its stopping test lets compute follow the difficulty
of a puzzle, and its learned weights can be read as the rules of a puzzle (Sec. 4.2). It trains fastest
with local objectives, and from few training puzzles (Sec. 4.3). Finally, we show that simple CRAM
energies whose weights are set by hand from the rules of each task solves all tested benchmarks at
least as accurately as every trained baseline, using at most three unique weights (Sec. 4.3).
 Tasks. We evaluate CRAM on four reasoning tasks: Sudoku-Extreme [62] (a set of hard 9 × 9
 Sudoku puzzles), Maze-Unique [36] (a set of 30 × 30 mazes whose answer is the unique path between
 two endpoints), N-Queens, and Graph Coloring [3, 11] (tasks that have many valid answers per input).
 Every task trains the pairwise energy of Eq. (1), and we specify how each task informs the structure
 of CRAM’s weights in Table 1. In no task is the energy told the rules of a puzzle.
Baselines. We test against HRM [62], Tiny Recursive Model (TRM) [38], Generative Recursive
Reasoning (GRAM) [3], Equilibrium Reasoners (EqR) [36], Flow Reasoning Models (FRM) [29],
Tiny Recursive Message Passing (TRMP) [52] and Energy Based Reasoners (EBR) [11]. Where
available, we retrain each baseline with its authors’ code and recipe on one H200 for timing and
score each baseline with its published inference setting.1 We score every method on the same test set
of each task, and no method chooses a setting or a checkpoint on the test set (App. C.1). CRAM
and each baseline trains with three seeds, and we report the mean over the three runs. We compare
only against models that produce the answer by their own recurrent computation. Methods that pass
learned costs to an external exact solver [10, 16, 17] are outside the scope of this work.
Training CRAM. CRAM weights are initialized to zero and are trained to minimizes the PL
objective of Eq. (5) at the labeled solutions using with SGD with momentum and a cosine schedule.
The priors of Table 1 enter as shared weights or, on Sudoku-Extreme, as digit relabeling of the
training boards. We take the final trained model and do not select a best performing checkpoint when
reporting CRAM’s results (App. C). Training runs finish by computing the stopping threshold from
the training solutions (their highest energy plus a small tolerance); reported fit times include this step.


1 The training code of TRMP is not released, so we reimplement TRMP from the recipe in its paper (App. C.8).

4.1    Comparison with looped models

High accuracy at small sizes and fast training. Does a CRAM energy that learns from solved
puzzles reach the accuracy of looped models, and at what cost? Table 2 compares CRAM with the
seven looped baselines on Sudoku-Extreme and Maze-Unique. On both tasks, CRAM is the most
accurate method, solving 99.996% of the Sudoku-Extreme test puzzles and every Maze-Unique test
maze. The most accurate looped model, TRMP, solves 99.87% and 99.23%. CRAM reaches this
accuracy with thousands of times less training time than every looped model. All results are trained
from examples except the gray rows of Table 2, which are a reference for an optimal CRAM energy
that is coded by hand from the puzzles explicit constraints (Sec. 4.3).

Efficient inference. Each method has a setting that allows us to vary its maximum allowed inference
budget: the number of reasoning steps (HRM, TRM, FRM), restarts (EqR), sampled trajectories
(GRAM, EBR), rollouts (TRMP), or for CRAM, the number of allowed search moves. By sweeping
across allowed inference budgets of each method on 10,000 Sudoku-Extreme test puzzles, we can get
a simple “scaling law” plot for inference compute, as shown in Fig. 2a. On this plot, CRAM uses
between 282× (against EBR) and 128,000× (against EqR) fewer FLOPs than the looped models when
compared at each baseline’s most accurate setting. CRAM reaches EBR’s top accuracy (99.86%)
using 1.9 × 109 FLOPs per puzzle vs EBR’s 5.4 × 1011 FLOPs. At max accuracy, CRAM solves
99.997% of the puzzles with a mean of 2.0 × 109 FLOPs per puzzle. Note that CRAM never returns
a wrong answer, it only “misses” where a search reaches its budget before any board passes the
stopping threshold.

Diverse valid solutions. N-Queens and graph coloring test whether the same energy finds many
different answers. Since both have many solutions per input, we follow GRAM and EBR [3, 11] and
report two scores in Table 3: the accuracy of a single answer (out of N = 1 or N = 20 chains), and a
valid-diversity score over 20 samples (Cov@20). CRAM’s learned energy performs competitively to
the best looped baselines across all metrics, despite training in seconds. Note that every Cov@20
value here comes from EBR’s evaluator, since GRAM publishes values from a different normalizer.

4.2    Advantages of explicit energy

Compute that adapts to difficulty. CRAM’s search stops at the first board whose energy is at most
the threshold fitted from the training solutions, which lets CRAM dynamically adjust its compute to
follow problem difficulty. When we bin the 10,000 test puzzles of Fig. 2a by the difficulty rating of
the dataset (Fig. 3a), the median compute of CRAM grows 22× from the easiest bin to the hardest.
In contrast, FRM spends the same compute for every puzzle and EBR, whose gradient-norm stopping
rule enables early stopping for Sudoku, barely changes compute needs across puzzle difficulty. Note
that the stopping test of CRAM is also reliable: it never stops at an incorrect board, whereas the
halting rules of HRM, TRM, EqR, TRMP and EBR each stop at an incorrect board on 0.05–3.54% of
the puzzles that they stop on (Table 4).
Inspectable learned weights. The nature of these logic puzzles mean we can read the rules of
the puzzle directly from CRAM’s learned weights (Fig. 2b). Despite receiving no task-informed
constraints for Sudoku, in every training run CRAM learned to put positive equal-digit costs on
exactly the 810 cell pairs that share a row, column or box, leaving all other costs zero. On Maze-
Unique, the 18 learned costs make each move of the search turn off one dead-end cell, which is
dead-end filling [50], a local rule that solves every perfect maze (App. C.4).

The puzzles evaluated in this work—Sudoku-Extreme [62], Maze-Unique [36], and the N-Queens and
graph-coloring sets of Baek et al. [3]—belong to a category of logic puzzles that CRAM’s pairwise
energy can represent exactly [35, 34]. In fact, Table 2 shows that a hand-set CRAM energy with
one unique weight value solves every Sudoku-Extreme test puzzle, and one with three values solves
every Maze-Unique test maze. High accuracy on these logic benchmarks therefore does not indicate
that a model can scale to harder tasks such as ARC-AGI-1 [62, 12], where the answer depends on
quantities that the model must compute from the visible board, such as each cell’s distance to the goal
in Maze-Hard. In App. F, we show how a simple latent extension of CRAM can learn nearly all of the
1,000 Maze-Hard test mazes, but learning such latent quantities in general and without foreknowledge
of the task’s design remains an important open research direction to scale these energies to harder
reasoning tasks.
Building efficient reasoning models that are applicable to general tasks is a central challenge in
AI research. Our results indicate that energy is a powerful framework for reasoning on constraint
puzzles, offering almost 300× faster inference and 1,000× faster training than looped models with
no loss in accuracy. Though the energy studied here is not a final answer for all reasoning tasks,
it showcases the incredible benefit that an explicit energy provides, and provides a promising path
towards data-efficient general reasoning capabilities.

B     Compute
Every experiment in this paper runs on one NVIDIA H200 GPU. Training jobs used GPUs on
unreserved nodes of a shared cluster, so other jobs could use a node’s other GPUs and CPU cores
which could affect reproducibility of timing a fit. Wherever CPU times are reported, they use
one thread of an Intel Xeon Platinum 8468 CPU. CRAM’s search moves run in Triton kernels
with one GPU warp per chain. A move changes one cell, so the kernel keeps the field of every
cell in memory and updates only the fields of the cells with a nonzero coupling to the moved cell.
The code, data preparation, run records and exported results behind every table and figure are at
https://github.com/Prefrontal-AI/CRAM.

C     Experimental protocols
C.1    Shared protocol

Every method, CRAM and each baseline, is trained three times under different seeds, and each
trained model is scored once on the test set. Where a published accuracy was selected on the test set,
we say so. The test sets have 412,786 puzzles (Sudoku-Extreme), 1,000 mazes (Maze-Unique), 1,240
and 18,466 rows (N-Queens, 8 × 8 and 10 × 10) and 255 and 192 graphs (graph coloring, 8 and 10
vertices).
When reproducing baselines, whenever our three-seed mean accuracy is more 2 points below their
published accuracy, a table shows the published value (dagger), but we still count that baseline’s
parameters and FLOPs ourselves. A baseline’s Params is the parameter count of its network: we
do not check its weights for zeros, so it is an upper bound on the nonzero parameters that CRAM’s
count reports.
CRAM inference is a focused Gibbs search with four chains (Sudoku-Extreme) or with N chains
(N =1 or 20 in Table 3; N-Queens and graph coloring), and zero-temperature single-cell moves
(Maze-Unique). In general, we use each baseline’s published inference setting when solving a task.

       • TRMP: the most confident of 64 noisy rollouts (the ACT model in Table 2).
       • TRMP’s lattice model (the § footnote of Table 2): 64 search chains, each restarted on a
         detected conflict, for at most 1,000 steps.

C.2     Operation counts and fit time

Reported FLOPs count the operations along all their sampling trajectories, stopping tests, selection
amongst samples, and decoding; it excludes losses and metrics computed from labels.
Every FLOP count in this paper, for CRAM and every baseline, uses the following convention:

        • A matrix product counts 2 FLOPs per multiply-add.
        • Every elementwise addition, subtraction, multiplication (also by a 0/1 mask), division,
          negation and absolute value counts 1, on floats or integers.
        • Every exp, log, square root, sigmoid, tanh and erf counts 1.
        • Comparisons, selections, min, max, argmax, mask logic, indexing, data movement, casts
          and random-number generation count 0. Arithmetic applied to a random number (scaling
          noise, a Gumbel transform) counts.

For the neural baselines, a PyTorch counter at the operator-dispatch level applies these rules to every
operator during inference, including gradient computations. CRAM’s moves use Triton kernels that
are invisible to the counter, so count their transcription line by line. CRAM’s code outside the kernels
is counted directly. A CRAM search counts FLOPs up until a move first passes its stopping test.
Reported fit times are the wall clock of one complete fit. The clock starts when the training data are
in host memory and stops when the inference-ready model is read, including its stopping statistics.
In total, it includes: transfer to the device, initialization, augmentation, every forward, backward
and optimizer step, compilation and CUDA-graph capture with their warm-up, and fitting stopping
thresholds and focus statistics from the training solutions. It excludes reading files, evaluation, saving
and module imports.
We report the warm time for CRAM, because its one-time start-up (0.2–0.9 s on one H200) is a large
part of a fit that takes milliseconds, but a negligible part of a baseline fit that takes hours.

        • Cold: the first fit in a fresh process, including one-time kernel loading, compilation and
          graph capture. CUDA context creation (0.4–0.7 s) is measured separately and excluded.
        • Warm: the median of seven further fits in the same process.

It was not within our compute budget to compute warm-starts for long baseline runs. Thus, each
baseline time is one cold fit on one H200 with an empty compilation cache. Its start-up (compilation,
kernel loading and the first update) takes 6 s to 16 min, at most 2.2% of the fit (3.5% for TRMP on
Maze-Unique).

C.4      Maze-Unique

Maze-Unique [36] (locuslab/EqR-data, subset maze-30x30-unique-1k) has 1,000 training and
1,000 test mazes of 30 × 30 cells, whose answers are a unique path from start to goal. Walls are
clamped off, the start and goal on, and PL of the binary path labels is applied at every open cell. We
chose the number of updates and the learning rate on 100 held-out training mazes: the fewest updates
(10, 20, 50 or 100), then the smallest rate (0.3, 1 or 3), at which every run solves at least 99 of them
with focused Gibbs search when trained on nested subsets of 10 to 900 mazes. The final fits use all
1,000 mazes.
Searching a maze starts with every open cell on. Each move sets a uniformly random cell that is
not at its lowest-energy value to that value, and the search stops at a fixed point (at most 290 moves
were used). From this start, each move turns off one dead-end cell, so the search performs dead-end
filling. This choice of start matters since the empty board has lower energy than the solution on every
development maze, and explains why exact solvers like toulbar2 can’t find this solution.

C.5   N-Queens

N-Queens uses GRAM’s data [3] without augmentation. An input is a solution with k queens removed
(k ∈ {5, 6, 7} on 8 × 8, {7, 8, 9} on 10 × 10), and an input with S solutions occurs S times, once
with each solution as its label (7,224 training and 1,240 test rows on 8 × 8; 108,234 and 18,466 on
10 × 10). Accuracy counts the test rows whose answer is a valid board that keeps every given queen.
Cov@20 sums, over distinct test inputs, the distinct valid solutions among 20 samples, capped at
min(S, 20), and divides by the sum of min(S, 20). This is the rule of EBR’s evaluator, not GRAM’s.
Training follows Table 5, with the gradient of each Jd divided by md /m̄, where md is the number of
cell pairs at offset d and m̄ its mean. Without this preconditioner, the long diagonals (2 pairs, against
162 for the shortest) barely train since there is insufficient data to learn long correlations.
Each chain starts from the given queens. A move picks a uniformly random free cell whose current
state has the higher field (any free cell if there is none) and draws its state with probability proportional
to exp(−gi (a)/u). A chain stops when every free cell has a margin of at least δ − 10−3 , or after
20,000 moves with its lowest-energy board; N =1 is the first chain, and N =20 the lowest-energy
board of 20 chains. A rule fixed before testing chose this recipe on 10 × 10 validation inputs; we
report it at both sizes, a choice made after seeing the test results (on 8 × 8, the rule’s own choice
scores 100.00% and 98.3% Cov@20, against 99.95% and 97.9%). To save GPU kernels, the fit builds
the pair-cost matrix from the offset costs as one product with a fixed 0/1 matrix instead of by indexing.
We timed this fit and tested the models of an indexing fit, whose parameters agree with it to within
10−7 of the largest parameter.


C.6   Graph coloring

Graph coloring uses GRAM’s graphs with chromatic number 3, each labeled with one 3-coloring in
canonical order. We score each graph once. Conflict counts the edges whose two vertices share a color,
summed over the test graphs. Cov@20 is the number of distinct proper colorings in canonical order
that 20 samples find, over the number of such colorings, summed over graphs (GRAM’s definition).
For each pair of vertices, the 3 × 3 cost table is an affine function of all the adjacency tokens of the
graph, so CRAM is not told which edge belongs to which pair (7,332 and 18,660 parameters; 869
and 475 are nonzero after training). Training follows Table 5.
Each chain starts from a uniformly random coloring and makes the focused moves of Sudoku-Extreme,
with focus threshold 0.45h̄. It stops at the first coloring on which every pair term and input cost is at
most its highest value on a training coloring.

the model; no gradient flows through the drawing of the negative. To keep the energies bounded, each
also penalizes their squares, as in Du and Mordatch [19].

each stored Sudoku chain together with its puzzle. Equilibrium propagation uses β = 0.01, at which

F      Extending CRAM to Maze-Hard

    Summary. The Maze-Unique energy, extended with 11 learned parameters and one hidden layer
    that settles in its own relaxation, solves 99.9% of the Maze-Hard test mazes after less than 30
    seconds of training on one GPU.

On Maze-Unique, the learned energy fills dead ends from the all-open board until only the unique
solution path is left (App. C.4). Maze-Hard mazes, on the other hand, contain loops that break
dead-end filling, because the choice between two sides of a loop depends on distances to the goal.
With nowhere in Eq. (1) to store these distance calculations, CRAM solves none of the Maze-Hard
test mazes.
To solve this, we can add one layer of hidden neurons z whose state falls by a fixed amount with each
step away from the goal, and let the pair costs of Eq. (1) read it. Training is the pseudolikelihood of
Eq. (5), as for the other tasks.

Training uses pseudolikelihood training as in Maze-Unique, and an adjoint solver for the hidden
parameters, without differentiating through either relaxation. The model has 29 total learned param-

Results. On the development mazes, each of three training runs solves 100 of 100. Trained on all
1,000 training mazes and scored once on the test set, each of three runs solves 999 of 1,000, with one
incorrect stop and no exhausted budget (Table 8). All three fail on the same maze, where a dead end
of six cells along the maze’s border, next to the path, stays on: its cells support each other through
the 3 × 3 pair costs, so the flow does not prune it. On the development mazes, learning an input per
token in place of the input at G solves 0 of 300, and one gate per pair in place of the normalized
window weights solves 0 of 100. A fit takes about half a minute on one H200, with the hidden-layer
relaxation and its adjoint each run as one fused Triton kernel per maze.
